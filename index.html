Overview

Lesson Structure & Objectives

This lesson traces the Voynich Manuscript across three layers of investigation — a popular video, a declassified NSA report by Brigadier Tiltman, and a comprehensive scholarly monograph by M.E. D'Imperio. Students will practise academic listening, close reading, critical thinking, and evidence-based discussion.

LevelB2 – C1

Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to: (1) summarise the known history of the manuscript; (2) compare and evaluate proposed decipherment theories; (3) distinguish between fact, inference, and speculation in academic texts; (4) use hedging language to express degrees of certainty.

Reading A: Tiltman's NSA Report (1967)

Brigadier John H. Tiltman was one of Britain's greatest codebreakers and spent over 25 years studying the Voynich Manuscript. His paper was declassified by the NSA in 2002 under the Freedom of Information Act. Read the summary below, then answer the questions.

Reading Summary — Tiltman (1967)

Tiltman identifies three published "solutions", all rejected by scholars. The first, by William Newbold (1921), claimed Roger Bacon wrote the manuscript and had possessed a telescope and microscope centuries before their supposed invention. Newbold's method — converting script strokes into Greek shorthand — was demolished by Professor John Manly, who showed the process was too subjective to yield a single, verifiable plain text. The second, by Feely (1943), produced medieval Latin that specialists rejected as inauthentic. The third, by Leonell Strong (1946), identified the author as Anthony Askham and produced medieval English equally unacceptable to scholars.

Tiltman's own analysis of 17 symbols led him to key observations: paragraphs almost always begin with specific symbols; there is no punctuation; some symbol groups behave as if they have inflectional (grammatical) endings; simple substitution of single symbols for letters is impossible because "languages simply do not behave this way." His tentative conclusion is that the text may represent a primitive synthetic universal language — a philosophical classification of ideas — similar to systems developed by Bishop Wilkins (1667) and Cave Beck (1657).

Deep Dive: D'Imperio's Monograph (1978)

Mary D'Imperio's The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma (NSA, 1978) is the most comprehensive scholarly study ever produced. Tiltman himself wrote the foreword, praising it as "far more comprehensive and more scholarly" than his own paper. Read the following thematic extracts, then complete the analysis tasks.

Extract A — On the Question of Authenticity

D'Imperio surveys scholarly opinion on whether the manuscript is genuine. Most researchers — including Tiltman, Panofsky, Elizabeth Friedman, and Carter — concluded it is not a hoax or a lunatic's doodlings, but rather "a homogeneous, creative work of a serious scholar who had something to convey." The only significant dissenter was Robert Brumbaugh, who argued it was a deliberate forgery designed to defraud Emperor Rudolph II. Even Brumbaugh, however, conceded there is likely an underlying text that may eventually be read.

Extract B — The Known History (a chain of evidence)

D'Imperio reconstructs a documented chain: the manuscript was at Rudolph II's court before 1608; it passed to Jacobus de Tepenecz (director of Rudolph's botanical gardens and alchemical laboratory) between 1608 and his death in 1622; then to an unknown person who bequeathed it to Joannus Marcus Marci; in 1665/6 Marci sent it to Athanasius Kircher in Rome. It disappeared from recorded history until Wilfrid Voynich found it at Villa Mondragone, Italy, in 1912. Voynich sold it; it passed through Hans P. Kraus (who valued it at $160,000), and was finally donated to Yale's Beinecke Library — where it remains today as MS 408.

Extract C — The Text Problem

D'Imperio identifies key textual phenomena that any theory must account for: (1) the script is internally consistent throughout — it follows rules; (2) there is no punctuation and spaces between "words" may not be reliable word boundaries; (3) certain symbol sequences repeat across the manuscript with characteristic frequencies, suggesting grammar; (4) the statistical distribution of symbols does not match simple substitution ciphers; (5) Captain Prescott Currier found evidence of at least two distinct "hands" (writers) and possibly two "languages" within the manuscript. This last point — if confirmed — rules out a hoax by a single person and strongly suggests a real collaborative text.

✍

Deep Dive Tasks — Work in pairs

  1. Fact vs. Inference vs. Speculation: Look at the five claims below. Classify each as a confirmed fact, an inference (a logical conclusion from evidence), or speculation. Be ready to justify your classification.
    • "The manuscript was written around 1420." — Fact / Inference / Speculation?
    • "Roger Bacon wrote the manuscript." — Fact / Inference / Speculation?
    • "The manuscript was in Rudolph II's court before 1608." — Fact / Inference / Speculation?
    • "The text contains meaningful content, not random nonsense." — Fact / Inference / Speculation?
    • "The manuscript may represent a universal language system." — Fact / Inference / Speculation?
  2. Hedging Language: D'Imperio and Tiltman both use careful hedging. Find one example of hedging in each extract above (e.g. "may", "likely", "suggests"). Rewrite each sentence making it more certain, then discuss: does that make it more or less accurate?
  3. The Two-Hands Problem: If Captain Currier's finding that there are two writers is correct, what does that tell us about the manuscript? List three implications.

Discussion: Connecting the Sources

The three sources we have used were produced 11 years apart — 1967 (Tiltman), 1978 (D'Imperio), and 2014 (Bax video) — yet they circle the same mystery. This phase asks students to reflect across all three.

🗣

Group Discussion — Choose 3 of the following

  1. The weight of consensus: Virtually every scholar agrees the manuscript is not a hoax. But does consensus make something true? What would it take to prove the manuscript is genuine versus a forgery?
  2. Who owns a mystery? The manuscript is a classified NSA document turned academic problem turned viral video. How does the audience (spies → scholars → YouTube viewers) change how the mystery is framed?
  3. The curse of past failure: D'Imperio notes that she "unwittingly retraced the steps of all her predecessors." Is there value in trying something that has already failed? Apply this to your own experience of learning.
  4. Bax vs. Tiltman: Bax (2014) uses proper nouns as anchors; Tiltman (1967) uses statistical symbol frequency. Which approach seems more scientifically rigorous? Which is more likely to eventually succeed?
  5. Your verdict: Based on all three sources, what do you believe the Voynich Manuscript is? Construct a position using evidence from at least two sources and hedging language.
Language Focus — Expressing Uncertainty (C1)

Academic writing and speaking on unresolved questions requires precise hedging. Practise these patterns: "The evidence suggests that…" · "It seems likely that…, although…" · "While X cannot be ruled out, the balance of evidence points to…" · "Tiltman's analysis indicates…, but this remains tentative" · "There is some reason to believe…"

✍

Writing Task (Optional — 150 words)

Write a short paragraph responding to this question: "After more than 600 years and dozens of failed attempts, is the Voynich Manuscript likely ever to be decoded? Should scholars continue trying?" Use evidence from at least two of the sources studied today. Use hedging language appropriately.
